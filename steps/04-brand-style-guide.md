# Step 4: Write your brand style guide

Step 4 of 34.

It comes after step 3, Write SOPs for every task, and before step 5, Secure your website and protect visitor data (coming soon).

## Why it matters

A brand style guide is the wiki page that says how your brand looks: your name, your logo, your colors, and your fonts.

Next to it, your content guidelines are the wiki page that says how your brand writes: your voice, who you write for, and the words you use.

They aren't only for your website: you use them for your social media, your emails, your ads, your videos, and everything else you publish.

Without them, every writer, designer and AI tool guesses, and your brand looks a little different everywhere.

With them, everything you publish looks and sounds like the same brand, no matter who creates it.

Write them once. Use them everywhere.

## Rules

Each rule says how to do it and why it matters.

Its level says whether every site must follow it (Mandatory) or whether you get to pick if it's relevant for you (Recommended).

### Keep how your brand looks in a brand style guide, and how it writes in content guidelines

Level: Mandatory

**Write how your brand looks in a brand style guide, and how it writes in content guidelines: two wiki pages. Writers and translators read both before working on text content, and designers read the brand style guide before any visual work. A color, size or rule that is not in them doesn't exist.**

**How to do it:** Create two pages in your wiki.

The "Brand style guide" holds how your brand looks: the logo and how to use it, the colors, the fonts, the sizes, and the image style.

The "Content guidelines" hold how your brand writes: the voice (how your brand sounds), who you write for, and the writing rules.

Both apply on your site, on social media and anywhere else your brand shows up.

Link to both from your setup file and from your shared instructions file for AI tools.

Writers and translators read both pages before they start, and designers read the brand style guide before any visual work.

When someone needs a color, a size or a rule that isn't in them, add it to the right page first, with the owner's yes, then use it.

**Why it matters:** Everything you publish, whether a page, a post or an ad, sounds and looks like the same brand, no matter who creates it.

Nobody invents a new shade of green on one page or one ad, so your brand doesn't slowly drift apart.

### Define your brand basics first

Level: Mandatory

**Before you start any visual work, define the brand basics: the name, the positioning line, the tagline, the logo versions, the colors, the fonts, and the image style.**

**How to do it:** Write each one in your brand style guide.

The name, spelled one way.

The positioning line: one sentence on what you offer and for whom.

The tagline: a short phrase people remember, if you want one.

The logo versions, and where each one is used, like the full logo in the header, the icon alone on small screens, and a square version for social media profile pictures.

Make sure your logo is original: check it with an image search like Google Lens, and if you start from a stock image or a ready-made design (a photo, drawing or design from a library like Shutterstock), read its license first.

The colors, each with its exact code, like #0C9F07, and what it's used for, like buttons or links.

The fonts, with the size for headings and for body text.

The image style, like "real photos of real people, no stock photos".

**Why it matters:** Every designer and AI tool works from the same brand basics, so every piece of design, whether it's a page, a banner or a 3D motion video, looks like it comes from the same brand, and you don't waste time redesigning it later.

### Spell your brand name and main keyword one way everywhere

Level: Mandatory

**The brand name and the main keyword are spelled exactly one way everywhere the brand shows up: on the site, on social media, in emails and in ads.**

**How to do it:** Write the exact spelling of your brand name in your brand style guide, with its capitals and spaces, like "Acme Studio", never "ACME studio" or "AcmeStudio".

Write the exact spelling of your main keyword, the search term your site is built around, in your content guidelines, like "e-bike", never "ebike" or "E-Bike".

Use that spelling in the text, the headings, the page titles and descriptions shown in search results, the share previews (the title, text and image shown when someone shares a page), the schema (code that tells search engines who you are), the alt text (the description of an image for people who can't see it), and the footer.

In file names and page addresses, use the same letters, in lowercase.

Every time before you publish a page, a post or an email, ask your AI to search it, and the whole site, for any other spelling.

**Why it matters:** If you spell it differently, it can look like a mistake, or like a different company.

People who search the other spelling may not find you, and search engines can't be sure both names are the same brand.

### Write one positioning page, and build all your content from it

Level: Mandatory

**Keep one positioning page, and write the top section of every page, every intro, every page's title and description in search results, and your social media bios and ads from it.**

**How to do it:** Positioning is how you describe what you offer, and why someone should pick you.

Write it on one page in your wiki, with: a one-line pitch, the problem you solve, the pain you save your customer, your promise, the steps to work with you, and what makes you better than the others.

When you write the top section of a page (the first thing a visitor sees), an intro, the title and description shown in search results, a social media bio or an ad, start from this page.

When your offer changes, update this page first, then update your content, using this page as the reference.

**Why it matters:** Every page, post and ad tells the same story about what you do, so people easily understand your offer.

You don't write the pitch from zero each time.

### Pick one voice for the whole brand

Level: Mandatory

**Pick one voice for the brand on purpose, and use it everywhere you write, never a mix.**

**How to do it:** Your voice can be, for example, casual, professional, friendly, or like a news article.

Pick it with the owner, and never let a writer or an AI pick it by default.

Name it in your setup file, and write it in full in your content guidelines, with two or three example sentences, and refine it as you write your first pages.

Write everything in that voice: your pages, buttons and error messages, but also your emails and social media posts.

**Why it matters:** A brand that changes voice from page to page, or from its site to its social media, feels like several companies stitched together, and people trust it less.

Writers and AI tools don't have to guess the voice, so you have fewer edits to do.

### Follow the same writing rules, whether your style is fun or serious

Level: Recommended

**Whether your brand writes in a casual, fun style or a serious, professional one, every page, post and email follows the same writing rules: answer what the reader searched for, give real examples and numbers, make no claim you can't prove, and use plain words.**

**How to do it:** In a casual style, still answer what the reader searched for, give real examples and numbers, and make no claim you can't prove.

For example, a bakery writes "Fresh out of the oven at 6 a.m.: 40 kinds of bread, every morning!" instead of "We're the coolest bakery in town!".

In a professional style, still write in plain words, so even beginners can understand.

Write this line at the top of the part of your content guidelines that describes your style: "Our style changes how we say things, never what we must get right."

**Why it matters:** A fun style is never an excuse to skip the facts, and a serious style is never an excuse for hard words.

So a fun post still answers the reader's question, and a professional page is still easy to read.

### Describe who you write for in your content guidelines

Level: Recommended

**Your content guidelines say who your brand writes for, the situation they're in when they find you, and which technical words they may not know.**

**How to do it:** Write who your brand talks to on your site, in your posts and in your emails, like "small business owners who have never built a website".

Then write the situation they're in when they find you, like in a hurry, looking for more detailed information, or expecting a quick solution.

Add a list of the technical words your readers may not know, each with the plain word or the short explanation to use.

**Why it matters:** Writers and AI tools write for a real person in a real situation, and this extra information helps them create better content.

### Write in plain, everyday words

Level: Mandatory

**Whatever your style, write in plain everyday words and short sentences that someone who is not native in that language can understand, never in a formal or legal tone.**

**How to do it:** Use the common word, like "use" instead of "utilize", or "buy" instead of "purchase".

Keep one idea per sentence.

Say in your content guidelines how long paragraphs are, like "one to three sentences".

Explain every technical word the first time it appears in a page, a post or an email.

Leave out sayings that only native speakers get, like "hit it out of the park".

Read the text out loud, and if you would never say a sentence that way, rewrite it.

**Why it matters:** The more people understand what you write and keep reading, on your site, in your emails or on social media, the better it is for your results, like more visitors who stay and buy, and the easier your content is to use.

On top of that, plain words are always easier to translate, so it will be easier to add more languages later.

### Choose one language variant and stick to it

Level: Mandatory

**The setup file states the regional variant of each language your brand writes in, and a page, a post or an email never mixes two variants.**

**How to do it:** Some languages have regional variants, like American or British English, or the Portuguese of Portugal or Brazil.

They spell some words differently, like "color" and "colour".

Write the variant for each language in your setup file, for example "English: American".

If it isn't there, ask the owner before you write.

Also write it in your content guidelines, so every writer reads it before creating any content.

You can also ask your AI to check each new page, post or email for spellings from the other variant before you publish.

**Why it matters:** Mixed spellings look like mistakes, and they make the whole brand look less serious.

### Decide how you address the reader, before you write

Level: Mandatory

**Decide, for each language, whether you address the reader formally or informally, before any text is written, and use it everywhere.**

**How to do it:** Some languages have two ways to say "you", like "tu" and "vous" in French, or "du" and "Sie" in German.

Decide which one your brand uses for each language, and write it in your setup file.

Also write it in your content guidelines, so every writer reads it before creating any content.

Use it on every page, in buttons, menus and error messages too.

Your social media posts can use the other one, if you write that down too.

If it isn't in the setup file, ask the owner before you write.

**Why it matters:** Switching between formal and informal within one brand sounds odd to native readers.

Changing it later means rewriting everything you published.

### Leave emojis out unless your content guidelines allow them

Level: Mandatory

**No emojis in headings or body text, unless your content guidelines say they are allowed, and where.**

**How to do it:** Decide once whether your brand uses emojis, and write the answer in your content guidelines.

If they are allowed, say where, like "only in social media posts" or "never in headings".

Before you publish a page, a post or an email, ask your AI to check it for emojis, and remove any your content guidelines don't allow.

**Why it matters:** AI tools often add emojis to headings and lists, and they can make a business look less serious.

One written decision saves you from removing them one by one.

### Pick one title separator and one punctuation style

Level: Mandatory

**Your content guidelines set one title separator for the website, and one way to write quotes, apostrophes and dashes everywhere your brand writes, and everything you publish follows them.**

**How to do it:** The title separator is the character between the page name and your brand name in a page title, like "Pricing | Acme" or "Pricing - Acme".

Pick one: a bar (|), a hyphen (-), a colon (:) or a slash (/).

Then pick one style for quotes (straight " or curly “), and one for apostrophes (straight ' or curly ’).

Decide which dashes are allowed: only the short hyphen, or the longer dashes too.

Write each choice in your content guidelines, with one example.

Before you publish, ask your AI to check every new page, page title, post and email against them.

**Why it matters:** Mixed separators and quotes look careless in search results, on your pages and in your posts.

Fixing them later means editing everything you published, one by one.

### Keep a list of banned words, each with its replacement

Level: Recommended

**Keep a list of the words and phrases you never use, each with what to write instead, including hype words, unless you can back them with proof the reader can check.**

**How to do it:** Add a "Banned words" section to your content guidelines.

Write one line each: the word, then what to write instead, like "utilize: use" or "world-class: say what makes it good".

Hype means big claims with nothing behind them, like "the best" or "revolutionary".

Include corporate and hype words, like "leader", "world-class", "synergy" or "revolutionary", unless you back them with proof the reader can check: a number, an award, a ranking or a source, like "rated 4.8 out of 5 by 1,200 customers on Google" instead of "the best".

Add every word the owner rejects, the day it's rejected.

Never remove a word without the owner's yes.

Before you publish, ask your AI to search every new page, post and email for the words on the list.

**Why it matters:** With this rule and this check, you avoid the same weak words coming back in everything you publish, so you don't need to fix them each time.

Readers have seen these words everywhere, so they skip them, or stop believing the rest of what you say.

### Keep the owner's corrections as before-and-after examples

Level: Recommended

**Keep every correction the owner makes to the text as a before-and-after pair, with the reason behind it, and reread them before you write anything new.**

**How to do it:** Create one page in your wiki called "Corrections".

For each correction, add the sentence before, the sentence after, and the reason in one line.

For example: "Before: We leverage synergies. After: We work together. Reason: plain words."

Before writing a new page, post or email, the writer, a person or an AI, rereads this page.

Use each reason as a lesson for what you write next, like "plain words", but don't paste the corrected sentence word for word into a new page, or every page starts to sound the same.

**Why it matters:** The same correction stops coming back in everything new you write, so the owner spends less time fixing text.

The reasons show the writer what the owner wants, beyond the one sentence.

### Make only claims you can prove

Level: Mandatory

**List the proof behind every claim your brand makes about itself, and what must stay true for it to hold, and never show a fake one.**

**How to do it:** List every claim you make about your business, on your site, in ads or on social media, like "500 customers", "since 2015" or "rated 4.8 out of 5".

Next to each, write the proof, and what must stay true for the claim to hold, like "only while our customer count is 500 or more".

Show numbers only from real, current data, read from your data files.

Never show fake testimonials (quotes from customers), invented badges or awards, fake countdown timers, made-up "trending" labels, or progress bars that start already filled.

Ask your AI to make the site hide a claim when its condition stops being true.

If you show a public counter, a number that grows like "1,200 people downloaded this guide", hide it until the real number is worth showing, like 50, and write that number in your content guidelines.

Always show the real number, never a higher one.

Set a reminder to check this list and update it on a regular basis, like every week, every month or every quarter, depending on your business. This check can be automated, so a script (a small program that does a task for you) or your AI runs it on a schedule and tells you what changed.

**Why it matters:** A fake claim found once can cost you the trust of everyone who sees it.

A small number, like "3 people downloaded this guide", can make your offer look like nobody wants it.

In many countries, fake reviews and fake urgency, like a countdown that isn't real, can also break consumer protection laws.

### Decide once whether you show reviews and ratings

Level: Recommended

**Decide once whether your brand shows testimonials or star ratings, on the site and elsewhere, and whether the site uses review markup, and write down why.**

**How to do it:** Review markup is code that can show star ratings next to your page in search results.

Decide with the owner whether you use each of them, on your site and in your ads or posts.

Add the decision and its reason to your state file.

Google doesn't show star ratings in its results for reviews of your own business that you put on your own site, so read Google's rules on review markup before you add it.

If you show reviews, show only real ones.

**Why it matters:** You, your team and your AI all follow the same decision, so nobody adds reviews to an ad or removes them from a page without asking.

The written reason saves you from discussing the same question again.

### Keep a brand kit folder

Level: Recommended

**Keep the brand files in one brand kit folder, and have every designer and AI tool use it before any visual work, for the site, social media or print.**

**How to do it:** Create one folder, for example `brand`, with the logo files, the color list, the icons and the fonts.

Link to it from your brand style guide.

Use only the files in this folder, never a logo saved from an old email or a website.

When a file changes, replace it in the folder.

**Why it matters:** Everything you publish uses the right logo and the right colors, so nobody has to redo a design made with an old file.

Someone new finds every brand file in one place, without asking or searching.

### Make a full set of logo files

Level: Recommended

**Prepare a full set of logo files from the brand style guide: a vector logo, favicons, a touch icon, and a default share image.**

**How to do it:** A vector logo is a file that stays sharp at any size, like an SVG file.

From it, make the favicon (the small icon in the browser tab and next to your site in Google's results): a square image of 48 by 48 pixels, or a multiple of it like 96 by 96, as Google asks.

Make the touch icon, the icon an iPhone shows when someone adds your site to their home screen, 180 by 180 pixels.

Make a default share image, the picture shown when someone shares a page that has no image of its own, 1200 by 630 pixels.

Put every file in your brand kit folder.

**Why it matters:** People recognize your site in their browser tabs, in Google's results and on their phone's home screen.

When someone shares one of your pages on social media, it shows your brand instead of a plain link with no picture, so your site looks finished and trustworthy.

### Write down what's off-brand, and why

Level: Recommended

**Write down the looks that are off-brand, and the reason behind every design decision the owner makes, so nobody "fixes" them back later.**

**How to do it:** Off-brand means a look that doesn't fit your brand, even if it looks good on its own.

Add an "Off-brand" section to your brand style guide.

Write one line for each look you don't want, with the reason, like "No diagonal stripes: they look like another brand."

Next to each design choice the owner makes, write the reason, like "Headings in capitals: the owner wants a bold look."

Read this section before you design anything new, like a page, an ad or a post.

**Why it matters:** A choice without a reason looks like a mistake to the next person, who then undoes it, and you lose time and money fixing it twice.

## Checklist

Run this checklist only when you audit a site that already exists.

Building a new site?

Don't run it yet.

Apply the rules above while you build, and the checks run at the pre-launch checklist step.

Check each line in your project's files or on the live site.

Mark a line yes only after that check, with who did it and the date.

Results are saved in your project's progress file, so nothing gets checked twice for no reason.

When you run the audit again, lines already marked yes are skipped, unless the files behind them changed since their verified date or the owner asks for a full re-check.
