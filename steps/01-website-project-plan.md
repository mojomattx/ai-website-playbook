# Step 1: Write your website project plan

Step 1 of 34.

It comes before step 2, Build your project knowledge base.

## Why it matters

A website project plan is what you decide and write down before you build anything: who the site is for, what it must achieve, which pages it needs, what it costs, and in what order you build it.

Every answer you write down here is one less guess later, for you, your team, and your AI.

Each rule below saves you time or money, or prevents a problem after launch.

## Rules

Each rule says how to do it and why it matters.

Its level says whether every site must follow it (Mandatory) or whether you get to pick if it's relevant for you (Recommended).

### Write a short mission

Level: Mandatory

**Before you build, write a short mission: who the site is for, the result you want (with clear numbers and deadlines), and what the site will not do.**

**How to do it:** Keep it to a few lines, like "For small business owners. Goal: 20 contact requests a month within 6 months. Not doing: an online shop."

This way you can check that every new page or feature serves one of your goals, and say which one.

**Why it matters:** Without a clear end goal, it's easy to add pages and features because you can build them, not because they serve a goal.

When you clearly list what the site is about, you or your AI can push back on new pages or features that don't move you toward your main goal.

### Write one short setup file

Level: Mandatory

**Every project has one short setup file, filled in before any work starts and read first by everyone, person or AI.**

**How to do it:** List the name, domain (your site's address, like example.com), site type, language, tech stack, voice, visual rules, infrastructure, and project-specific exceptions.

The tech stack is the tools and software the site is built with.

The infrastructure is where the site is hosted and the services it runs on.

Keep it short, and link to longer wiki pages (the pages that explain how your project works) instead of copying them.

**Why it matters:** Everyone starts from the same facts, so nobody guesses the language, the voice, or the stack.

### Name one owner who decides

Level: Mandatory

**Name one owner who decides scope, voice, approval, and publishing.**

**How to do it:** Write the owner's name in the setup file.

Everyone else, people and AI, proposes and waits for the owner's yes.

**Why it matters:** When nobody owns the decision, work gets done twice or published without a check.

### Get the owner's yes before doing anything outside the rules

Level: Mandatory

**Anything outside the written rules, or any clash between two rules, is written down first, then approved by the owner before it is applied.**

**How to do it:** When a task needs something the rules don't cover, write it down and send it to the owner.

Apply it only after the owner's yes.

The same goes when two of your rules say different things, and nothing in your rules says which one wins.

Stop, write down both rules, and send them to the owner.

Wait for the answer, then add it to your wiki, so the question doesn't come back.

**Why it matters:** The owner sees every exception before it reaches the site.

Then it's added to the wiki, so it's there for the next person.

When two rules disagree, a guess can pick the wrong one, and fixing it later costs more than asking now.

### Research what people search for

Level: Mandatory

**Before you build, research what people search for.**

**How to do it:** Find out what people search for on your topic, so you can map which pages to build and in what order.

Look at the search results in Google and in a few AI assistants.

Learn from what they recommend, and see what you can add.

Write the date next to each keyword or search intent you check, so you always know when it was last checked.

A search intent is what the person who searched wanted to find, like a price, a how-to, or a place to buy.

**Why it matters:** Knowing what people search for tells you which pages to build first.

### Pick search terms you can rank for

Level: Mandatory

**Pick search terms you can realistically rank for, which means showing up near the top of the results.**

**How to do it:** Short, broad searches are often taken by big and established sites.

Pick longer, more precise searches where fewer sites compete.

For example, a small bakery will struggle to rank for "cake", but it can rank for "gluten-free birthday cake in [city]".

**Why it matters:** A new site rarely beats big and established sites on the broadest terms.

### Give people a reason to come back

Level: Recommended

**Write down what brings people to the site the first time, and what brings them back.**

**How to do it:** Write two short lists.

First, what brings a visitor the first time, for example a guide that answers a question they searched for.

Second, what brings them back, for example a price list or a calendar they check every week, or a newsletter that links back to your site.

Plan at least one page or feature that gives people a reason to come back.

**Why it matters:** If people visit once and never come back, you always have to find new visitors, from search or from ads.

Visitors who come back bring steady traffic, so you depend less on search and ads.

### Define every template before you build

Level: Mandatory

**Before you build, define every template (the shared layout that pages of the same kind are built from): what it holds, how it gets built, how it gets updated, and where its data comes from.**

**How to do it:** A template is the shared layout that every page of the same kind is built from, like every product page or every blog post.

Make a table with one row per template, like homepage, service page, or article.

Give the table four columns: what the page holds, how it gets built, how it gets updated, and where its data comes from.

Define every template you can think of before you start building any of them.

**Why it matters:** The table gives the AI a structure, so it puts the right content in the right place.

It doesn't have to rethink the structure and the design every time it builds a page.

It also makes it easier to change every page that shares a template, all at once.

### Reuse what already exists

Level: Recommended

**Set up a new project by reusing and adapting what already exists: rules, templates, SOPs (an SOP is a process made of actions to take, like a checklist), and tools.**

**How to do it:** Before writing anything new, check your existing rules, templates, and tools, and copy what fits.

Write something new only when nothing fits, then save it so the next project can use it.

**Why it matters:** Writing the same thing again wastes time, and two versions of one rule soon disagree.

### Big site? Plan it on paper first

Level: Recommended

**Big site? Plan it on paper first, and get the owner's yes before anything gets built.**

**How to do it:** This applies when the site will have about 50 pages or more, facts that change after publishing, or many pages made from one template.

A small site with a few pages whose facts never change can skip it.

Write down your answers to these questions:

Can we rank, or do big sites own the main searches?

What templates do we need?

What makes the pages built from each one go out of date?

What can a script (a small program that does a task for you) update for us?

How many hours a week will maintenance take, and who does it?

How will we know if a script stops working?

Which automatic checks (facts, links) will we set up in the first week, before we publish many pages?

For example: "Match page, 104 of them, data from the results feed, a new score makes it wrong, a script updates it within an hour."

The rules on templates, search research and maintenance cost in this step show how to answer each one.

**Why it matters:** On a big site, one problem found after launch will impact dozens of pages at once.

### Measure what the site will cost to maintain

Level: Mandatory

**Before you build at scale, measure what the site will cost to maintain.**

**How to do it:** List what must be checked again after launch, how often, and how long each check takes.

Time it on a small test batch of pages, never guess.

Multiply to get the daily hours at full size.

Size the project for the people you actually have, on their worst week.

**Why it matters:** A site that needs more maintenance than your team can give goes out of date, and outdated pages lose trust and rankings.

### Pick the tech stack that fits your site

Level: Mandatory

**Pick the tech stack that fits what the site needs and suits your situation best.**

**How to do it:** Your tech stack has three parts.

The builder: the tool you make the pages with, like WordPress, Webflow or Astro.

The hosting: the service that keeps your site online.

The services it plugs into: like forms, payments or email.

List what the site must do (sell online, take bookings, publish articles), then check each tool's features page and keep only the tools that do all of it.

If two stacks both fit, pick the one that suits your situation best:

the one your team already knows,

a very popular one, with plenty of guides online and many people who can take over the work,

the one with the best security,

or the one that needs the least maintenance.

**Why it matters:** Someone else can take the site over without learning new tools first.

With a popular stack, you find help fast and can hire from a large pool of people, so problems get fixed sooner.

The most secure stack, or the one with the least maintenance, saves you time on fixes later.

### Keep the setup lean

Level: Mandatory

**Keep the setup lean, with the fewest vendors and moving parts.**

**How to do it:** Vendors are outside companies whose services the site uses.

Before you add a service, check whether a tool you already use can do the job.

**Why it matters:** Every extra service is one more thing to pay for, update, and fix.

### Select your hosting wisely

Level: Mandatory

**Select your hosting wisely: pick one that fits your budget, the traffic you expect, and where your visitors are.**

**How to do it:** Estimate how many visitors a month you expect at launch, and in a year.

If you can't tell yet, pick a plan you can upgrade later without moving your site.

Check that the plan can handle that traffic: read its limits (visits, storage, and bandwidth, the amount of data your pages send to visitors each month) and prices on the host's own pages, and note the date, since they change.

Some hosting is cheap but can't handle thousands of visitors.

If your visitors are local, pick a host with servers close to them.

For example, a site for Thailand hosted in Singapore is fine.

If your visitors are all over the world, pick a host with servers around the world, so each visitor gets the page from a server near them.

**Why it matters:** Your site stays fast and online when traffic grows, without a surprise bill.

Pages load faster when the server is close to the visitor, and fast pages keep visitors on the site.

### Have an outside developer review your stack

Level: Recommended

**Have an outside developer review a one-page stack proposal before you build.**

**How to do it:** Write one page: what the site needs, the stack you picked, and why.

Ask a developer who isn't on the project to look for gaps.

**Why it matters:** Someone outside the project catches problems that are cheap to fix now and expensive after launch.

### Know what the site costs each month

Level: Recommended

**Before you sign up for anything, estimate what the site will cost each month, then log what you actually pay.**

**How to do it:** List every paid service the site needs: hosting, domain, tools, email, and so on.

Write the monthly price of each, taken from the vendor's own page.

Turn yearly prices into monthly ones (divide by 12), then add them all up.

Once you start paying, log each expense the day it's paid, with the date, the service, the amount and what it's for, gifted credit included.

**Why it matters:** You don't sign up for tools you can't afford, and you know how much the site must bring in to pay for itself.

The log shows what the site really costs, so nothing gets forgotten.

### Choose a country domain or a generic one on purpose

Level: Mandatory

**If your site is for one country, choose on purpose between a country domain and a generic one.**

**How to do it:** A country domain, like .fr or .de, tells search engines the site is for that country.

A generic domain, like .com, works for any country.

Pick a country domain if your site will only ever be for people in that country.

Pick a generic domain if you might add other countries later.

**Why it matters:** Changing it later means moving the whole site to a new address.

### Check your domain name before you register it

Level: Mandatory

**Before you register a domain name, check that it's available, easy to spell, and fairly priced.**

**How to do it:** Check that the name is available, using the search box of any domain registrar (a company that sells domain names).

Say the name out loud to someone and ask them to spell it, and if they get it wrong, pick another.

Compare the price at two or three registrars, then register it.

**Why it matters:** Changing the domain later means moving every page to the new address, and losing part of the place your pages have earned in search results.

### Put up a coming-soon page the day you buy the domain

Level: Recommended

**Put up a simple coming-soon page the day you buy the domain.**

**How to do it:** Publish one page with the site name and one line on what the site will offer.

Make sure robots.txt (the file that tells search engine robots where they may go) doesn't block it.

Make sure the page has no noindex tag (a tag that tells search engines to leave a page out of their results).

**Why it matters:** Search engines start finding the domain and indexing it (adding its pages to their results) while you build the rest.

Search engines take time to trust a new domain, so the sooner it's indexed, the sooner that trust starts to build.

### Estimate the work part by part

Level: Recommended

**Estimate the effort for each part of the work to get a realistic launch date.**

**How to do it:** Split the work into design, content, tools, SEO setup (SEO means optimizing your website, and the content you post on other platforms, so it's easier to find when people search, whether on Google, social media, AI tools or AI agents), and testing, and estimate each part on its own.

Add them up and set the date from the total.

**Why it matters:** Some parts of a website take much longer than people expect, like writing all the content.

When you estimate each part separately, you spot those slow parts before you start, so you can set a realistic launch date.

### Put the job in writing before anyone starts

Level: Recommended

**If you work with other people, give them a clear list of specs (exactly what the job must include and deliver) and a timeline, agree on the cost, and have them confirm and sign a written agreement before they start.**

**How to do it:** Send each contractor or freelancer the specs and the timeline in writing, and agree on the cost.

Ask them to confirm they can do the job within that timeline and for that cost.

Write the specs, the timeline, the cost, and the penalties if any of these aren't respected into a written contract or agreement.

They start only once it's signed.

**Why it matters:** You both agree on what gets done, by when and for how much, before any work starts, and the penalties give everyone a reason to stick to it.

### Ask everyone to log their changes

Level: Recommended

**Anyone who works on the site logs every change they make.**

**How to do it:** Ask each contractor or freelancer to keep one list of every change they make to the site.

**Why it matters:** If something breaks, the log shows what changed.

### Keep a "now or later" list

Level: Recommended

**Keep a written "now or later" list, and push heavy features to later phases.**

**How to do it:** Heavy features are things like accounts (visitors log in), databases (where the site stores data such as users or orders), and live features (things that update in real time, like a chat).

Write each one on the list as now or later.

**Why it matters:** The first version goes live sooner, and the list shows what was left for later on purpose, not forgotten.

### Keep a separate page for ideas

Level: Recommended

**Keep a separate page for ideas in your wiki for anything you're not sure about yet.**

**How to do it:** Create one page called "Ideas" in your wiki, and write each idea there in one line, with the date you added it.

Never put those ideas on your plan or to-do list, so it only holds work you've decided to do.

When you decide to do an idea, move it to your plan, and add the decision to the decisions list in your state file (one file with your project's decisions, facts, and paused items, set up in step 2).

Put open questions on their own page, numbered, with the date you asked them.

**Why it matters:** Your to-do list stays short and clear, so you don't spend time on work you haven't decided to do, and good ideas aren't lost.

Nobody builds from an idea by mistake, thinking it was decided.

### Decide what to do first

Level: Recommended

**Put every task on one ordered list, and work from the top, one task at a time.**

**How to do it:** Step 1: before you start, write every task on one list.

Step 2: mark each task now or later, and keep anything on your "later" list of heavy features (accounts, databases, live features) for later.

Step 3: cross out any task that serves none of the goals in your mission.

Step 4: put the "now" tasks in this order.

First, anything that shows a visitor something wrong, like a wrong price or a broken page.

Second, the pages for the searches you can realistically win, from your research.

Third, on a site that is already live, the pages that almost rank.

To find them, open Google Search Console or Bing Webmaster Tools (both free, once your site is connected), go to the performance report, and sort your pages by average position (where the page shows up in search results on average, 1 is the top).

Pages at about position 8 to 20, the bottom of the first page or the second page, come first.

Last, small fixes that only change how something looks or reads.

Step 5: finish the task at the top before you start the next one.

**Why it matters:** You always know what comes next, and nothing is left half done.

A page that already almost ranks gives you more for less work than a page that starts from nothing.

### Keep a content tracker of every page

Level: Recommended

**Keep a content tracker: one file in your project folder that lists every page of your site, built or planned.**

**How to do it:** Create one file called `content-tracker.csv` in your project folder, and have your AI keep it up to date.

A CSV file is a simple table: it opens in Google Sheets or Excel, and GitHub shows it as a table.

Add one row per page, for the pages that already exist and for the ones you plan to build.

List each page here, not on your task list, so every page appears in one place only, in the same order.

Give it these columns: URL (the page's address), template, silo or category, target keyword, status (planned, written, live), date published, date last updated, next review date, and task link (the link to the page's task in your task manager, like ClickUp or Notion).

A silo, or category, is a group of pages on one topic, with one main page that links to all of them, like a "Cakes" page with one page for each kind of cake.

Put the planned rows in the order you set when you decided what to do first, grouped silo by silo: each silo's main page first, then every page in it.

Build your next pages from the top of the planned rows, and link every page in a silo together before you start the next silo.

Each time a page is written, goes live, or gets updated, change its status and dates in the file.

**Why it matters:** You always know which page to build next, so no time is lost deciding, and no page gets built twice or forgotten.

It also helps visitors find a complete topic instead of scattered pages, and you never have to go back later to add missing links.

And the review dates tell you when each page needs a check, so your pages don't go out of date.

### Work in phases, each with an exit condition

Level: Recommended

**Run the project in phases, each with a clear exit condition.**

**How to do it:** Write down each phase and what must be true for it to end.

Open the next phase only when the previous one runs mostly without the owner.

**Why it matters:** Each phase ends with something that works, instead of many things half done.

### Build one page of each template first

Level: Mandatory

**Build one page of each new template first, from start to finish, and check it before you build the rest.**

**How to do it:** For each new template, build one page from start to finish, check it against the rules in this playbook for that kind of page (content, SEO, the pre-launch checklist), and get the owner's yes.

For example, build one product page completely before you list 200 products on your site, and one blog post before you publish the next ones.

Write down what you fixed in the steps you follow to build that kind of page, so every next page gets it right.

Then build the rest of the pages that use that template.

**Why it matters:** A mistake caught on the first page gets fixed once, not 200 times on every page built from that template.

### Replace every placeholder before you publish

Level: Recommended

**If you start from a ready-made design or a design library, mark all its placeholder content and replace it before you publish.**

**How to do it:** Placeholder content is the sample text, images and logos that come with the design.

Mark every placeholder with the same word you can search for, like `TODO-REPLACE`: in the text itself, and in the file name of sample images and logos (todo-replace-logo.png).

Add a note to your to-do list: replace every placeholder before you publish.

Before you publish, search your site's files or pages for that word.

No results means you're done.

**Why it matters:** A forgotten sample text or the design's demo logo can go live and get indexed by search engines.

It makes the site look unfinished to visitors, and search engines keep showing it until they visit the page again.

### Launch with fewer, really good pages

Level: Recommended

**Launch with fewer pages that are really good, rather than many average ones.**

**How to do it:** Pick the pages that matter most for your goals, make each one complete, and add the rest after launch.

**Why it matters:** Good pages earn links and trust.

Many thin pages can pull down how search engines judge the whole site.

### Launch as soon as the site meets its main mission

Level: Recommended

**Launch the website as soon as it has enough to meet your main mission, like selling things or getting people to reach out to buy your service.**

**How to do it:** At this stage the site isn't ready yet, so add this launch check to your project's plan or to-do list now.

Ask whoever builds the site, a person or an AI, to tell you as soon as the site passes every point of the launch check below.

The launch check:

Look at the main goal you wrote down in your mission at the start.

Check that the site has all the main information a visitor needs to do that one thing.

Check that the site is technically done, which means it meets the rules of the security and speed steps.

Check that the SEO is technically done, which means it meets the rules of the technical SEO and sitemap steps, so search engines can index the site.

Check that your [wiki](02-project-knowledge-base.md) and SOPs are written, so everything you add later follows them.

Run the pre-launch checklist: every page you launch is complete, and the site passes a free site audit, like Ahrefs Webmaster Tools (free on your own verified site), before it goes public.

Fix every error the audit reports.

When the builder tells you every point is done, launch.

You can add features and improve the design later.

**Why it matters:** The site starts doing its job sooner, selling or bringing in clients, instead of waiting for features it doesn't need yet.

You don't spend time polishing features and design before you know what visitors actually use.

### Build in phases, and don't launch 15 features at once

Level: Recommended

**Build in phases. You shouldn't launch 15 features at once.**

**How to do it:** Pick what goes into this phase: one feature or several, a new section of the website, or a set of things to improve.

How much you put in depends on the size of the work.

Step 1: spec each thing in the phase, which means writing down in a few lines what it is, who it's for, and what it should do.

Step 2: work on it until it's live.

Step 3: get feedback from users.

Ask a few customers or visitors what they used and what was missing, by message, email or a short call.

If you track your traffic, check how many people visit the new parts and whether they use them (click, sign up, contact you).

Step 4: improve it based on what you learned.

Step 5: then move on to the next phase, and start again at Step 1.

**Why it matters:** You don't spend weeks building features nobody uses.

Each phase is shaped by what real users need, so your time and money go where they bring results.

When something goes wrong, you know which change caused it, because only this phase's changes went live.

## Checklist

Run this checklist only when you audit a site that already exists.

Building a new site?

Don't run it yet.

Apply the rules above while you plan, and the checks run at the pre-launch checklist step.

Check each line in your project's files.

Mark a line yes only after that check, with who did it and the date.

Results are saved in your project's progress file, so nothing gets checked twice for no reason.

When you run the audit again, lines already marked yes are skipped, unless the files behind them changed since their verified date or the owner asks for a full re-check.
